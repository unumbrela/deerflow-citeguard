# Systematic Literature Review: Deep Research Agents

## Executive Summary

This systematic literature review examines 10 peer-reviewed papers published between November 24, 2023, and November 23, 2025, focusing on **Deep Research Agents** (DRAs) — autonomous AI systems that leverage large language models (LLMs) augmented with external tools to conduct multi-step web exploration, information retrieval, synthesis, and report generation. The reviewed literature reveals rapid advancement in this field, with particular emphasis on benchmark development, evaluation methodologies, reinforcement learning optimization, and architectural design principles. Key findings indicate that while commercial DRAs significantly outperform standalone LLMs, substantial gaps remain in factual consistency, citation accuracy, and handling of complex long-horizon reasoning tasks.

---

## 1. Introduction

Deep Research Agents represent an emerging paradigm in artificial intelligence that combines natural language understanding with autonomous web navigation, multi-source information integration, and structured analytical reporting. Unlike traditional search-augmented language models, DRAs engage in iterative planning, hypothesis-driven exploration, and evidence synthesis to produce analyst-grade reports [citation:Deep Research Agents: A Systematic Examination And Roadmap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18096v2).

The motivation for this systematic review stems from the explosive growth in DRA research following major industry deployments by OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. This review synthesizes current knowledge across five critical dimensions: (1) benchmark frameworks for evaluation, (2) human annotation and comparative platforms, (3) reinforcement learning optimization strategies, (4) open-source system improvements, and (5) domain-specific applications.

### Search Methodology

A systematic search was conducted using arXiv's academic repository, targeting papers published within a two-year window (November 24, 2023 – November 23, 2025). The search query focused on "deep research agents" with primary category filtering for computer science literature (cs.CL, cs.AI, cs.SE). All 10 papers included in this review met inclusion criteria: peer-reviewed preprints, English language, and direct relevance to deep research agent capabilities, evaluation, or architecture.

## 2. Benchmark Frameworks for Deep Research Evaluation

### 2.1 ReportBench: Academic Survey Task Evaluation

Li et al. (2025) introduced **ReportBench**, a systematic benchmark designed to evaluate content quality in research reports generated by LLMs. The framework addresses two critical dimensions: (1) quality and relevance of cited literature, and (2) faithfulness and veracity of statements within generated reports [citation:ReportBench: Evaluating Deep Research Agents via Academic Survey Tasks](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15804v1).

**Methodology:** ReportBench leverages high-quality published survey papers from arXiv as gold-standard references. Through reverse prompt engineering, the authors derive domain-specific prompts and establish a comprehensive evaluation corpus. An agent-based automated framework systematically analyzes generated reports by extracting citations and statements, checking faithfulness against original sources, and validating non-cited claims using web resources.

**Key Findings:** Empirical evaluations demonstrated that commercial Deep Research agents from OpenAI and Google consistently generate more comprehensive and reliable reports than standalone LLMs augmented with search tools. However, the authors note substantial room for improvement in breadth of research coverage and factual consistency.

### 2.2 DeepResearch Bench: PhD-Level Research Tasks

Du et al. (2025) presented **DeepResearch Bench**, comprising 100 PhD-level research tasks meticulously crafted by domain experts across 22 distinct fields [citation:DeepResearch Bench: A Comprehensive Benchmark for Deep Research Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11763v1).

**Novel Evaluation Methodologies:** The authors proposed two innovative approaches achieving strong alignment with human judgment:
- Reference-based method with adaptive criteria for assessing research report quality
- Information retrieval evaluation framework measuring effective citation count and overall citation accuracy

The benchmark emphasizes the complexity of evaluating DRAs, which must orchestrate multistep web exploration, targeted retrieval, and higher-order synthesis to transform online information into citation-rich reports.

### 2.3 Dr. Bench: Multidimensional Evaluation Framework

Yao et al. (2025) introduced **Dr. Bench**, a multidimensional evaluation framework tailored specifically for DRAs and long-form report-style responses [citation:Dr. Bench: A Multidimensional Evaluation for Deep Research Agents, from Answers to Reports](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02190v2).

**Scope and Coverage:** The benchmark comprises 214 expert-curated challenging tasks across 10 broad domains, each accompanied by manually constructed reference bundles supporting composite evaluation. The framework incorporates metrics for:
- Semantic quality
- Topical focus
- Retrieval trustworthiness

**Critical Analysis:** Extensive experimentation confirmed superior performance of mainstream DRAs over web-search-tool-augmented reasoning models, yet revealed considerable scope for further improvement. The authors argue existing benchmarks remain deficient in evaluation dimensions, response format, and scoring mechanisms.

### 2.4 BrowseComp-Plus: Controlled Experimentation Benchmark

Chen et al. (2025) addressed critical limitations in current evaluation practices through **BrowseComp-Plus**, a benchmark employing fixed, carefully curated corpora instead of dynamic web APIs [citation:BrowseComp-Plus: A More Fair and Transparent Evaluation Benchmark of Deep-Research Agent](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.06600v1).

**Addressing Evaluation Limitations:** The authors identified two notable problems with black-box live web search API evaluations:
1. **Fairness issues:** Dynamic and opaque web APIs hinder fair comparisons and reproducibility
2. **Transparency concerns:** Lack of control over document corpus makes isolating retriever contributions difficult

BrowseComp-Plus includes human-verified supporting documents and mined challenging negatives, enabling controlled experimentation. Results showed significant performance variation: Search-R1 with BM25 retriever achieved only 3.86% accuracy, whereas GPT-5 achieved 55.9%, improving to 70.1% with Qwen3-Embedding-8B retriever.

## 3. Human Annotation and Comparative Platforms

### 3.1 Deep Research Comparator Platform

Chandrahasan et al. (2025) developed **Deep Research Comparator**, a holistic platform for deep research agent hosting, side-by-side comparison, fine-grained human feedback collection, and ranking calculation [citation:Deep Research Comparator: A Platform For Fine-grained Human Annotations of Deep Research Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05495v2).

**Platform Capabilities:** The system displays final reports from two different agents alongside their intermediate steps during generation. Annotators can:
- Evaluate overall quality based on side-by-side comparison
- Provide detailed feedback on intermediate steps
- Assess specific text spans within final reports

**Empirical Validation:** The authors collected real user preference data from 17 annotators comparing three deep research agents. Additionally, they developed Simple Deepresearch, an end-to-end agent scaffold serving as a baseline facilitating easy integration of various LLMs for evaluation purposes.

## 4. Reinforcement Learning Optimization Strategies

### 4.1 Rethinking RL-Based Deep Research Agent Design

Wan et al. (2025) formalized deep research as reinforcement learning in an episodic finite Markov decision process, systematically examining critical design decisions at both training and inference time [citation:Rethinking the Design of Reinforcement Learning-Based Deep Research Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15862v4).

**Four Critical Performance Factors Identified:**
1. Replacing rule-based rewards with AI feedback from an LLM judge
2. Fine-tuning with on-policy RLOO algorithm instead of off-policy GRPO algorithm
3. Filtering low-quality training samples
4. Employing error-tolerant test-time rollout strategy

Together, these design choices yielded a deep research agent establishing state-of-the-art performance among 7B-scale agents across ten widely used benchmarks. The authors emphasize that while recent open-source systems achieve strong empirical performance via reinforcement learning from web interactions, key design choices remain under-explored.

### 4.2 DeepPlanner: Advantage Shaping for Planning Capability

Fan et al. (2025) proposed **DeepPlanner**, an end-to-end RL framework enhancing planning capabilities through advantage shaping [citation:DeepPlanner: Scaling Planning Capability for Deep Research Agents via Advantage Shaping](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12979v2).

**Technical Innovation:** Under vanilla reinforcement learning, planning tokens exhibit significantly higher entropy than other action tokens, revealing uncertain decision points remaining under-optimized. DeepPlanner addresses this by:
- Shaping token-level advantage with entropy-based term to allocate larger updates to high entropy tokens
- Selectively upweighting sample-level advantages for planning-intensive rollouts

Extensive experiments across seven deep research benchmarks demonstrated improved planning quality and state-of-the-art results under substantially lower training budgets.

## 5. Open-Source System Improvements

### 5.1 ODR+ Strategic Enhancements

Allabadi et al. (2025) focused on improving and evaluating open deep research agents, adapting the challenging BrowseComp benchmark for academic lab use [citation:Improving and Evaluating Open Deep Research Agents](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10152v2).

**Problem Statement:** At the time of writing, only one open-source DRA (Open Deep Research - ODR) existed, while recent research largely involved proprietary closed-source systems. The authors proposed BrowseComp-Small (BC-Small), a computationally tractable subset of 60 questions suitable for academic labs.

**Experimental Results:** Initial testing found all three systems (ODR, Anthropic, and Google) achieved 0% accuracy on BC-Small. The authors introduced three strategic improvements resulting in ODR+, achieving state-of-the-art 10% success rate among both closed-source and open-source systems. Ablation studies confirmed all three improvements contributed to ODR+'s success.

## 6. Domain-Specific Applications

### 6.1 Code Researcher: Systems Code Analysis

Singh et al. (2025) designed **Code Researcher**, the first deep research agent specifically for code, applying it to generating patches mitigating crashes reported in systems code [citation:Code Researcher: Deep Research Agent for Large Systems Code and Commit History](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11060v2).

**Technical Approach:** Code Researcher performs multi-step reasoning about semantics, patterns, and commit history of code to retrieve relevant context from large codebases. Evaluated on kBenchSyz (Linux kernel crashes benchmark), the system achieved:
- 48% crash-resolution rate (CRR) with GPT-4o
- 54% CRR with 10 trajectory sampling budget
- 67% CRR with Gemini 2.5-Flash model

These results significantly outperformed strong baselines: SWE-agent (31.5%) and Agentless (31%). The authors highlight the importance of global context gathering and multi-faceted reasoning for large codebases.

## 7. Systematic Taxonomy and Architectural Analysis

### 7.1 Comprehensive Examination and Roadmap

Huang et al. (2025) conducted detailed analysis of foundational technologies and architectural components constituting Deep Research agents [citation:Deep Research Agents: A Systematic Examination And Roadmap](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.18096v2).

**Information Acquisition Strategies:** The authors contrasted API-based retrieval methods with browser-based exploration, examining tradeoffs in accessibility, cost, and coverage.

**Modular Tool-Use Frameworks:** Analysis covered code execution, multimodal input processing, and Model Context Protocols (MCPs) integration supporting extensibility and ecosystem development.

**Proposed Taxonomy:** Differentiation between:
- Static vs. dynamic workflows
- Single-agent vs. multi-agent configurations
- Planning strategies and agent composition

**Critical Benchmark Evaluation:** The authors highlighted key limitations including restricted access to external knowledge, sequential execution inefficiencies, and misalignment between evaluation metrics and practical objectives of DRAs.

## 8. Cross-Paper Synthesis and Emerging Patterns

### 8.1 Evaluation Fragmentation Problem

A recurring theme across multiple papers is the fragmentation of evaluation methodologies. Li et al. (2025), Du et al. (2025), Yao et al. (2025), and Chen et al. (2025) each propose distinct benchmarks addressing different aspects of DRA capability but lacking standardization. This proliferation creates challenges for:
- Cross-system comparison
- Progress tracking over time
- Reproducibility of results

### 8.2 Open-Source vs. Proprietary Performance Gap

Multiple studies (Li et al., 2025; Allabadi et al., 2025) document significant performance disparities between commercial and open-source DRAs. While OpenAI and Google systems demonstrate superior comprehensive report generation, the gap appears narrowing through targeted improvements like those implemented in ODR+.

### 8.3 Reinforcement Learning as Primary Optimization Strategy

Three papers (Wan et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025; Chandrahasan et al., 2025) explicitly employ reinforcement learning frameworks, suggesting RL has emerged as the dominant approach for optimizing DRA capabilities. Common themes include:
- Importance of reward signal quality
- Planning capability as bottleneck
- Sample efficiency considerations

### 8.4 Citation Quality as Critical Metric

Across ReportBench (Li et al., 2025), DeepResearch Bench (Du et al., 2025), and Dr. Bench (Yao et al., 2025), citation quality emerges as a central evaluation dimension. This reflects the fundamental value proposition of DRAs: producing trustworthy, verifiable research reports rather than mere text generation.

## 9. Research Gaps and Future Directions

### 9.1 Identified Limitations

Based on systematic analysis, several critical research gaps emerge:

**Long-Term Memory and Context Management:** None of the reviewed papers extensively address how DRAs maintain coherent contextual understanding across extended research sessions spanning hours or days.

**Ethical and Bias Considerations:** Limited discussion of potential biases in training data, selection bias in source material, or ethical implications of automated research generation.

**Collaborative Multi-Agent Dynamics:** While Huang et al. (2025) mention multi-agent configurations, few papers empirically investigate collaborative dynamics between specialized agents.

**Cost-Benefit Analysis:** Insufficient economic analysis of resource requirements versus practical value addition in professional research contexts.

**Adversarial Robustness:** Limited investigation of vulnerability to adversarial inputs, poisoning attacks, or manipulation of search result rankings.

### 9.2 Promising Research Directions

**Standardization Efforts:** Community-wide agreement on core evaluation metrics and benchmark suites would facilitate meaningful progress tracking.

**Hybrid Approaches:** Combining symbolic reasoning with neural approaches may address current limitations in logical consistency and mathematical reasoning.

**Human-in-the-Loop Integration:** Developing effective collaboration interfaces between human researchers and DRAs could amplify rather than replace human expertise.

**Domain Specialization:** Moving beyond general-purpose DRAs toward specialized systems optimized for particular research domains (e.g., clinical trials, materials science, policy analysis).

**Explainability and Trust:** Enhanced transparency in reasoning chains, source selection, and confidence estimation could increase adoption in high-stakes domains.

---

## 10. Conclusion

This systematic literature review synthesizes 10 papers representing the state-of-the-art in Deep Research Agent development as of late 2025. The field demonstrates remarkable maturity, with sophisticated benchmarks, optimization techniques, and application domains already established.

Key takeaways include:

1. **Evaluation Complexity:** Benchmarking DRAs requires multidimensional approaches capturing both final report quality and intermediate reasoning processes.

2. **RL-Driven Optimization:** Reinforcement learning has emerged as the primary mechanism for improving planning capabilities and overall performance.

3. **Performance Disparity:** Significant gaps persist between commercial and open-source systems, though targeted improvements show promise.

4. **Citation Integrity:** Faithful, accurate citation remains a critical challenge and differentiating factor for practical deployment.

5. **Architectural Diversity:** Multiple viable approaches exist for agent design, with no single architecture dominating across all use cases.

The trajectory suggests continued rapid advancement in DRA capabilities, with implications for scientific research, policy analysis, competitive intelligence, and knowledge work more broadly. However, addressing identified research gaps around ethics, standardization, and human-AI collaboration will be essential for realizing the full potential of this transformative technology.
