# Systematic Literature Review: AI Scientists

**Date**: 2026-10-26
**Papers surveyed**: 10
**Scope**: arXiv search query "AI scientists", category cs.AI, published between 2023-10-27 and 2025-10-26
**Citation format**: APA 7th edition

## Executive Summary

The literature on AI Scientists reveals a rapidly evolving field transitioning from conceptual frameworks to practical implementations. Between late 2023 and late 2025, research progressed from proposing benchmark tests for AI scientist capabilities to deploying fully autonomous systems capable of generating peer-review-accepted papers. The surveyed papers collectively demonstrate that while AI scientist systems have achieved remarkable milestones—including workshop-level paper acceptance and integration with over 2,700 scientific tools—significant bottlenecks remain in implementation capability, verification procedures, and transparency of automated workflows. The field shows strong convergence around the need for standardized platforms, rigorous benchmarking, and enhanced execution capabilities to enable AI scientists to produce groundbreaking scientific discoveries.

## Methodology

This review surveyed 10 arXiv papers retrieved on 2026-10-26 using the query "AI scientists", filtered to category cs.AI, published between 2023-10-27 and 2025-10-26. Papers were sorted by relevance and the top 10 were included. Metadata extraction (research question, methodology, key findings, limitations) was performed by language-model agents, with cross-paper synthesis performed by the lead agent.

**Limitations of this review**: arXiv preprints are not peer-reviewed; some included papers may not reflect their final published form. Coverage is limited to arXiv — papers published directly in venues without arXiv preprints are not represented.

## Themes

### Theme 1: Automated Scientific Discovery Systems

The most prominent theme across the literature is the development of end-to-end automated scientific discovery systems. Lu et al. (2024) introduced the foundational AI Scientist framework capable of generating novel research ideas, writing code, executing experiments, and producing full scientific papers at less than $15 cost per paper. Building on this, Yamada et al. (2025) presented AI Scientist-v2, which achieved the first entirely AI-generated peer-review-accepted workshop paper through progressive agentic tree-search methodology. These systems represent a fundamental shift from human-led to AI-autonomous research workflows, with the capability to iteratively develop ideas in an open-ended fashion similar to the human scientific community.

The evolution from the initial AI Scientist to version 2 demonstrates rapid progress in eliminating reliance on human-authored code templates and generalizing across diverse machine learning domains. The integration of Vision-Language Model feedback loops for iterative refinement of figure content and aesthetics further enhances the quality of AI-generated research outputs.

### Theme 2: Benchmarking and Evaluation Frameworks

A critical thread in the literature focuses on establishing rigorous benchmarks to evaluate AI scientist capabilities. Yin (2024) proposed seven "Turing Tests" for AI scientists, designed to assess whether an AI agent can make groundbreaking discoveries without relying on human-generated knowledge. These tests span historical scientific developments from inferring the heliocentric model to developing sorting algorithms, providing a comprehensive evaluation framework.

Luo et al. (2025) introduced BAISBench, a domain-specific benchmark for evaluating AI scientists on omics data-driven biological discovery. This benchmark includes cell type annotation across 15 expert-labeled datasets and 193 multiple-choice questions from 41 published studies, with comparison to graduate-level bioinformaticians. The results indicate that while current AI scientists demonstrate substantial potential, they fall short of fully autonomous biological discovery, highlighting the need for continued development in domain-specific capabilities.

### Theme 3: Implementation and Verification Bottlenecks

Multiple papers identify implementation capability as a fundamental bottleneck preventing AI scientists from achieving groundbreaking results. Zhu et al. (2025) provide extensive quantitative evidence that the primary limitation lies in the capability to execute requisite verification procedures. Their systematic evaluation of 28 research papers generated by five advanced AI scientist systems reveals that despite progress—including acceptance at ICLR 2025 workshop and ACL 2025—AI scientists lack the execution capabilities needed for rigorous experiments and high-quality scientific papers.

Xie et al. (2025) expand on this analysis through a prospect-driven survey, identifying key bottlenecks and critical components required for scientific agents capable of producing ground-breaking discoveries. While noting that LLM-based AI scientist systems are taking the lead in scientific research, they acknowledge identifiable limitations that must be addressed before human-level AI scientists capable of uncovering previously unknown phenomena can become reality.

### Theme 4: Platform Infrastructure and Standardization

The literature emphasizes the need for standardized platforms to democratize and scale AI scientist development. Gao et al. (2025) present ToolUniverse, an open platform that standardizes AI-tool interactions through a declaration system covering over 2,700 scientific tools and 130+ research skills. The platform automatically refines tool interfaces, generates new tools from natural language descriptions, and composes tools into agentic workflows, demonstrated through case studies in target assessment, chemical strategy, and clinical-trial safety.

Zhang et al. (2025) address the dissemination challenge by introducing aiXiv, a next-generation open-access ecosystem designed specifically for AI-generated research. Traditional journals and conferences rely on human peer review, making them difficult to scale, while existing preprint servers like arXiv lack rigorous quality-control mechanisms. aiXiv's multi-agent architecture enables research proposals and papers to be submitted, reviewed, and iteratively refined by both human and AI scientists, with experiments demonstrating significant enhancement in the quality of AI-generated research.

### Theme 5: Transparency and Failure Mode Detection

A critical emerging theme concerns the hidden pitfalls and failure modes in AI scientist systems. Luo et al. (2025) identify four potential failure modes: inappropriate benchmark selection, data leakage, metric misuse, and post-hoc selection bias. Their controlled experiments assessing two prominent open-source AI scientist systems reveal several failures across a spectrum of severity that can be easily overlooked in practice.

The paper makes a crucial recommendation: journals and conferences should mandate submission of workflow artifacts alongside AI-generated papers, as access to trace logs and code from the full automated workflow enables far more effective detection of failures than examining the final paper alone. This transparency requirement addresses the concern that "the more you automate, the less you see"—highlighting the paradox where increased automation can obscure critical methodological flaws.

### Theme 6: Theory Convergence and Interpretability

Fu et al. (2025) explore a foundational question: when two AI models are trained on the same scientific task, do they learn the same theory or different theories? Using Hamiltonian-Lagrangian neural networks as AI scientists, they mechanistically interpret and quantify theory agreement across many training seeds. Their findings reveal that AI scientists tend to converge in the theories they learn as more systems are provided in training data, though they sometimes form distinct groups corresponding to different theories.

The research demonstrates strong seed dependence of training dynamics and final learned weights, controlling the rise and fall of relevant theories. Notably, AI scientists switch from learning Hamiltonian theory in simple setups to Lagrangian formulation when more complex systems are introduced, providing insights into how AI scientists develop and select theoretical frameworks.

## Convergences and Disagreements

**Convergences**: The surveyed papers show strong agreement on several points. First, all acknowledge that current AI scientist systems, while impressive, have not yet achieved groundbreaking scientific discoveries on par with human researchers (Zhu et al., 2025; Xie et al., 2025; Luo et al., 2025). Second, there is consensus that standardized platforms and tool integration are essential for scaling AI scientist capabilities (Gao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025). Third, multiple papers agree that transparency through workflow artifact submission is necessary for quality assurance (Luo et al., 2025; Yamada et al., 2025).

**Disagreements**: The literature shows some tension regarding the timeline and feasibility of achieving human-level AI scientists. While Xie et al. (2025) suggest that "a human-level AI Scientist capable of uncovering previously unknown phenomena may soon become reality," Luo et al. (2025) present more cautious findings from BAISBench showing AI scientists fall short of fully autonomous biological discovery even in narrow domains. Additionally, there is implicit disagreement about whether the primary bottleneck is implementation capability (Zhu et al., 2025) versus transparency and failure detection (Luo et al., 2025).

## Gaps and Open Questions

The collective literature reveals several significant gaps. First, none of the papers explicitly address the ethical implications of fully autonomous AI scientists, including questions of research integrity, authorship attribution, and potential misuse. Second, while benchmarks exist for specific domains (BAISBench for biology, Turing Tests for general discovery), there is no unified evaluation framework that spans multiple scientific disciplines. Third, the cost-effectiveness claims—such as Lu et al.'s (2024) "$15 per paper"—lack detailed breakdowns of computational resources and environmental impact.

Fourth, the literature does not adequately address how AI scientists should handle conflicting results or negative findings, which are crucial components of scientific progress. Fifth, while platform infrastructure is emphasized, there is limited discussion of interoperability standards between different AI scientist systems and existing scientific software ecosystems. Finally, the question of how AI scientists should be integrated into existing scientific institutions, funding mechanisms, and career structures remains unexplored.

## Per-Paper Annotations

### Fu et al. (2025)

**Research question**: When two AI models are trained on the same scientific task, do they learn the same theory or different theories?
**Methodology**: The authors propose MASS and use Hamiltonian-Lagrangian neural networks as AI Scientists, trained on standard physics problems across many seeds to simulate different AI scientist configurations. They mechanistically interpret and quantify theory agreement using these neural networks.
**Key findings**:
- AI scientists tend to converge in the theories they learn as more systems are provided in training data
- AI scientists sometimes form distinct groups corresponding to different theories
- AI scientists switch from learning a Hamiltonian theory in simple setups to a Lagrangian formulation when more complex systems are introduced
- There is strong seed dependence of training dynamics and final learned weights, controlling the rise and fall of relevant theories
- The neural networks can aid interpretability and be applied to higher dimensional problems
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Gao et al. (2025)

**Research question**: How can AI scientists be built more easily through a shared platform that unifies tools, data, and analyses?
**Methodology**: The authors present ToolUniverse, an open platform that standardizes AI-tool interactions through a declaration system where every tool specifies its purpose, typed schema, and backend-agnostic invocation format, covering over 2,700 scientific tools and 130+ research skills.
**Key findings**:
- ToolUniverse standardizes how AI scientists identify and call tools through an AI-tool interaction standard
- The platform applies its standard to more than 2,700 scientific tools and over 130 research skills spanning ML models, datasets, APIs, and scientific packages
- It automatically refines tool interfaces, generates new tools from natural language descriptions, and composes tools into agentic workflows
- Case studies demonstrate ToolUniverse was used to create AI scientists that carried out end-to-end analyses in target assessment, chemical strategy, and clinical-trial safety
- The open-source ToolUniverse is available at https://aiscientist.tools
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Luo, E. et al. (2025)

**Research question**: To what extent can AI scientists extract meaningful biological insights from real experimental omics data?
**Methodology**: The authors introduce BAISBench, a benchmark evaluating AI scientists on real single-cell transcriptomic datasets through two tasks: cell type annotation across 15 expert-labeled datasets and scientific discovery via 193 multiple-choice questions from 41 published studies, with comparison to six graduate-level bioinformaticians.
**Key findings**:
- Current AI scientists fall short of fully autonomous biological discovery
- AI scientists already demonstrate substantial potential in supporting data-driven biological research
- BAISBench serves as a practical benchmark for characterizing current capabilities and limitations of AI scientists in biological research
- The benchmark provides a human performance baseline through graduate-level bioinformaticians completing the same tasks
- BAISBench can guide development of more capable AI scientists and help biologists identify AI systems for real-world research workflows
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Luo, Z. et al. (2025)

**Research question**: What are the potential failure modes in contemporary AI scientist systems that could undermine the integrity and reliability of their research outputs?
**Methodology**: Identifies four potential failure modes (inappropriate benchmark selection, data leakage, metric misuse, and post-hoc selection bias) and designs controlled experiments to isolate each failure mode while assessing two prominent open-source AI scientist systems.
**Key findings**:
- Four potential failure modes are identified: inappropriate benchmark selection, data leakage, metric misuse, and post-hoc selection bias
- Assessment of two prominent open-source AI scientist systems reveals several failures across a spectrum of severity
- These failures can be easily overlooked in practice
- Access to trace logs and code from the full automated workflow enables far more effective detection of failures than examining the final paper alone
- Journals and conferences should mandate submission of workflow artifacts alongside AI-generated papers
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Lu et al. (2024)

**Research question**: Can frontier large language models independently perform complete scientific research and communicate findings without human intervention?
**Methodology**: The paper presents a comprehensive framework for fully automatic scientific discovery where LLMs generate novel research ideas, write code, execute experiments, visualize results, write full scientific papers, and run simulated review processes. The framework was applied to three ML subfields (diffusion modeling, transformer-based language modeling, learning dynamics) with each paper developed at less than $15 cost.
**Key findings**:
- The AI Scientist can produce papers that exceed the acceptance threshold at top ML conferences as judged by an automated reviewer
- Each research idea is implemented and developed into a full paper at a cost of less than $15 per paper
- The automated reviewer achieved near-human performance in evaluating paper scores
- The system can iteratively develop ideas in an open-ended fashion, acting like the human scientific community
- The approach brings transformative AI agent benefits to the entire research process of AI itself
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Xie et al. (2025)

**Research question**: How far are AI scientists from changing the world and reshaping the scientific research paradigm?
**Methodology**: A prospect-driven survey that comprehensively analyzes current achievements of AI Scientist systems, identifying key bottlenecks and critical components required for scientific agents capable of producing ground-breaking discoveries.
**Key findings**:
- LLM-based AI Scientist systems are now taking the lead in scientific research
- AI-generated research papers have been accepted at ICLR 2025 workshop
- Current AI Scientist systems have identifiable limitations and bottlenecks
- A human-level AI Scientist capable of uncovering previously unknown phenomena may soon become reality
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Yamada et al. (2025)

**Research question**: Can an agentic AI system autonomously produce peer-review-accepted scientific papers without human-authored code templates?
**Methodology**: The study introduces The AI Scientist-v2, an end-to-end agentic system using progressive agentic tree-search methodology managed by a dedicated experiment manager agent. The system was evaluated by submitting three fully autonomous manuscripts to an ICLR workshop, with AI reviewer components enhanced via Vision-Language Model feedback loops for iterative refinement.
**Key findings**:
- The AI Scientist-v2 successfully produced the first entirely AI-generated peer-review-accepted workshop paper
- One manuscript exceeded the average human acceptance threshold, marking the first instance of a fully AI-generated paper navigating peer review
- The system eliminates reliance on human-authored code templates and generalizes effectively across diverse machine learning domains
- Enhanced AI reviewer integration with VLM feedback improved iterative refinement of figure content and aesthetics
- The open-sourced system enables unprecedented scalability in research productivity and accelerates scientific breakthroughs
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Yin (2024)

**Research question**: Can an AI agent conduct scientific research independently and make groundbreaking discoveries without relying on human-generated knowledge?
**Methodology**: Proposes seven benchmark tests inspired by historical scientific developments to evaluate an AI agent's ability to make groundbreaking discoveries across various domains, including inferring the heliocentric model, discovering laws of motion, deriving differential equations, inferring Maxwell's equations, inventing numerical methods, discovering Huffman coding, and developing sorting algorithms.
**Key findings**:
- Seven benchmark tests are proposed to assess AI scientist capabilities
- Tests are designed to evaluate discoveries that were groundbreaking in their historical time
- AI agents are provided with interactive libraries or datasets without access to human knowledge about target discoveries
- Passing the majority of these seven tests would indicate significant progress toward building an AI scientist
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Zhang et al. (2025)

**Research question**: How can we address the challenge that AI-generated research lacks appropriate venues for dissemination due to fragmented and closed publication ecosystems?
**Methodology**: Introduction and evaluation of aiXiv, a next-generation open-access platform with multi-agent architecture that enables research proposals and papers to be submitted, reviewed, and iteratively refined by both human and AI scientists, with API and MCP interfaces for seamless integration.
**Key findings**:
- Traditional journals and conferences rely on human peer review, making them difficult to scale for AI-generated research
- Existing preprint servers like arXiv lack rigorous quality-control mechanisms
- aiXiv's multi-agent architecture allows iterative revision and reviewing by human and AI scientists
- Experiments demonstrate aiXiv significantly enhances the quality of AI-generated research proposals and papers
- The platform provides API and MCP interfaces for seamless integration of heterogeneous human and AI scientists
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract

### Zhu et al. (2025)

**Research question**: What is the fundamental bottleneck preventing AI Scientists from producing groundbreaking achievements in computer science?
**Methodology**: The authors provide extensive quantitative evidence from existing benchmarks in complex engineering tasks and systematically evaluate 28 research papers generated by five advanced AI Scientist systems to identify the implementation gap.
**Key findings**:
- The fundamental bottleneck for AI Scientists lies in their capability to execute the requisite verification procedures
- Current AI Scientist systems lack the execution capabilities needed to execute rigorous experiments and produce high-quality scientific papers
- Despite progress, AI Scientist has yet to produce a groundbreaking achievement in computer science on par with automated scientific tools
- Generated research reports from AI Scientists have gained acceptance at ICLR 2025 workshop and ACL 2025
- The paper calls for the community to bridge the implementation gap
**Limitations**: Not stated in abstract
